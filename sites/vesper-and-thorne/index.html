§The trade

A bell is the only instrument you cannot retune by turning a peg. You tune it by taking metal away, and metal does not go back. So we measure for a week, cut for an hour, and listen for the rest of our lives.

IFire

First we build
the hole a bell
will fill.

A bell mould is loam: sand, clay, horse manure and goat hair. We have tried modern things. The goat hair wins.

We sweep two shapes with a board cut to the bell’s profile, the inside and the outside, and bake them hard. The gap between them is the bell. Everything we know lives in the curve of that board; change it by the thickness of a fingernail and the note moves.

Fri21:40 · the wait

We pour on Fridays, after dark. Dark, because you judge bronze by its colour. Friday, because a bell wants the weekend to cool.

Fri21:52 · the pour

Four minutes. Nobody speaks. Seventy-eight parts copper to twenty-two of tin goes in at eleven hundred and fifty degrees, in one unbroken stream, or not at all.

Fri22:06 · the quiet

Then we bury it and go to the pub. Nobody will know what we made until Monday. You get used to this. It takes about twenty years.

WaitPourQuiet

On Monday we dig it up, knock on it, and hold our breath.

What comes out of the ground is black, ugly, and a little sharp of the note we want. That is on purpose. You can lower a bell’s voice by cutting. Nobody has ever found a way to raise one.

“I have poured four hundred and six bells and I have been certain about none of them. The day you are certain, you stop looking at the colour.”

IIIVoice

A bell does not
sing one note.
It sings five.

Strike a bell and you hear a chord: five partials, stacked. Out of the mould they disagree. Tuning is the business of bringing them into line by shaving the inside wall, a different band for each, on a lathe older than anyone who works it.

Looking up the mouth. Each bright ring is where a partial was brought in.

As cast: five partials, five opinions.

“You stop when it stops arguing with itself. Cut once more after that and you have made a very handsome garden ornament.”

IVThe ring

Six bells.
720 ways.

England’s oddest music has no tune, only arithmetic. Six ringers, six ropes, and the bells swap places one pair at a time, never repeating a row, until every order has been rung. This is the first sixty changes of Plain Bob Minor. Follow the orange line; that is the path one bell takes through the others.

VOut there

A bell’s working life starts the day it goes out of the door. We get about nine months with each one. The parish gets the next ten generations.
