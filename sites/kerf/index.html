late light

On the last ferry of the season, the harbour pilots read the tide the way their grandfathers did: by the colour of the water.

The pilots of Marlow keep no logbook. What they know about the tide they keep in the hands, in the way a rope pays out against a cleat, in the colour the estuary turns an hour before the turn. Visitors ask how they learn it. They shrug and point at the water.

By October most of the pleasure boats are gone. The harbour is left to the working hulls and to the light, which arrives late and low and lays itself across the mud flats like a sheet of brass.

It was in this light that we met Ide Marrow, sixty-one, who has taken the evening ferry out past the bar every night for thirty-four years and has never once, she says, been late.

“The tide is never late either,” she says. “We just stop paying attention to it.”
