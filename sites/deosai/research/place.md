## 1. Basics

### Location
- An alpine plateau in the western Himalaya, east of Nanga Parbat and close to the Central Karakoram range, in Gilgit-Baltistan, Pakistan. [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Lies between Skardu District and Astore District. [ref] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · [peer] https://www.pakbs.org/pjbot/papers/1538435113.pdf
- No permanent human habitation on the plains, because of the weather and remoteness. [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- The surrounding Deosai Mountains run about 190 km (120 mi) from the Indus bend at Bunji to the Karcha (Suru) River. [ref] https://www.britannica.com/place/Deosai-Mountains (via search snippet) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_Mountains

Notes for the writer:
- No source reconciles these. My reading (inference, not a sourced fact): field scientists measuring the plateau/park polygon get 1,600–1,800 km²; official and heritage documents give 3,000–3,626 km², which may include surrounding valleys. Do not state that explanation as fact.

### National park: when, why, who
- Notified as a national park in 1993, with the primary purpose of protecting the Himalayan brown bear (*Ursus arctos isabellinus*). [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/ · [primary] https://fwegb.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/GB-FWP-Brown-Bears-Survey-2022-09-03.pdf
- Exact date given by the GB Forest Dept: 14 May 1993. [primary] https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/ (only source for the day and month)
- Who declared it — phrasing differs: "Government of Pakistan" (UNESCO); "government of the Northern Areas", through a joint effort of the Northern Areas Forest Department and the Himalayan Brown Bear Project, later incorporated as the Himalayan Wildlife Foundation (HWF https://hwf.org.pk/?p=2025). Northern Areas is the former name of Gilgit-Baltistan.
- Managed today by the Gilgit-Baltistan Forest, Parks & Wildlife Department (Forest, Wildlife & Environment Department). [primary] https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/ · https://hwf.org.pk/?p=2025
- Handover from HWF to the department — sources disagree on the year:
  - "following 2005" the department took full control (HWF's own page) https://hwf.org.pk/?p=2025
  - department "has managed the park since 2006" (Wikipedia) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
  - HWF ran a summer field camp 1993–2006 (Nawaz et al. 2008) https://www.currentconservation.org/recovery-of-brown-bears-in-northern-pakistan/
  - HWF supported the Forest Department 1993–2004 (Dawn 2015) https://www.dawn.com/news/1164658
  - HWF field staff inducted into the department in 2001 (Wikipedia, HWF article) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayan_Wildlife_Foundation
- Management plans: first written by HWF in 2005; a later one developed by WWF and approved by the GB Wildlife Management Board in 2016; reviewed and revised by EvK2CNR under a UNDP project (2018–20). https://www.evk2cnr.org/en/p-e-p/other-publications/deosai-national-park
- IUCN protected-area category: Wikipedia says Ib (wilderness area); the GB Forest Dept page says "Category I". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/

### UNESCO status
- On Pakistan's **Tentative List** (not inscribed as a World Heritage Site). Date of submission 12 April 2016; natural criteria (ix) and (x); reference 6115; submitted by the Government of Pakistan, Directorate General of Archaeology. [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- UNESCO's own disclaimer: the content of a tentative-list entry is the responsibility of the State Party, not UNESCO. Same URL. So write "Pakistan's submission to UNESCO says…", not "UNESCO says…".
- A 2017 Pamir Times report said UNESCO had "accepted" the list; that only means it was placed on the tentative list. https://pamirtimes.net/?p=63603
- National Geographic phrases it as nominated for World Heritage status in 2016. https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/beautiful-natural-wonders
- Still on Pakistan's tentative list as revised 11 May 2026 (Pakistan National Commission for UNESCO document; via search snippet). https://pncu.gov.pk/SiteImage/Downloads/Tentative%20List%20-%20updated.docx

### The "second-highest plateau" claim — how sources phrase it
- Wikipedia: the Deosai Plains are the second highest plateau in the world after the Changtang Tibetan Plateau. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
- Melis et al. 2023 (peer-reviewed): the second highest plateau in the world, highest after the Changtang Tibetan Plateau. https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/15/7/1800
- Shaheen et al. 2019 (peer-reviewed): the world's 2nd highest plateau. https://www.pakbs.org/pjbot/papers/1538435113.pdf
- EvK2CNR, Dawn (2023), Friday Times (2015): "second highest plateau in the world". https://www.evk2cnr.org/en/p-e-p/other-publications/deosai-national-park · https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028 · https://thefridaytimes.com/16-Jan-2015/the-last-sanctuary
- GB Forest Dept (official): only "one of the highest plateaus in the world". https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/
- UNESCO entry, Britannica and Nature Geoscience do **not** make the "second-highest" claim at all.
- Recommendation: write "often described as the world's second-highest plateau, after Tibet's Changtang". It is a widely repeated description, not a measured ranking, and the most official sources avoid it.

## 2. Names

### "Deosai"
- Shina: دیوسای٘ — from *deo* ("giant") + *sai* ("shadow"), read as "shadow of the giant" or "land of the giant". [ref] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · same gloss in Dawn 2015 https://www.dawn.com/news/1178524
- Gujjar lore, as recorded by travel writer Salman Rashid: the Gujjars say *Dev Vasai*, "land settled by the giant" (the Dev). [news] https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945 · https://thefridaytimes.com/17-Jan-2014/land-of-the-giant · also "Dev Vasai / land of the giant" in https://www.dawn.com/news/1164658
- Singular vs plural: Wikipedia credits Salman Rashid with coining the English "Land of the Giant" in the early 1990s; Rashid argues the popular plural "Land of Giants" is a mistake because the legend has one giant. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945 . National Geographic and most media use the plural "Land of the Giants". https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/beautiful-natural-wonders
- Vigne (1835/1842) spelled it **Deotsuh** and guessed the name meant "the lake of the Deo, or spirit", because the plain floods at the thaw. Vol. 2 p. 219 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002 . Rashid says Vigne was wrong about the meaning. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945

### Urdu
- دیوسائی ; the park is دیوسائی نیشنل پارک. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park

### Legends
- The farming giant (Gujjar tale, told by Salman Rashid): a simple-minded wandering giant took the flat grassland for farmland and began ploughing at the spring thaw; a fox warned him the summer lasts barely eight weeks; the snow came before he could harvest and he fled. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
- Djinns and fairies also feature in local tales; Rashid's 1995 book has a chapter titled "Through the Land of Djinns". Same URL.
- The gold-digging "ants": Herodotus (*Histories* 3.102–105) described fox-sized furry "ants" that throw up gold-bearing sand in a far province of the Persian empire. French ethnologist Michel Peissel (*The Ants' Gold*, 1984) argued these were the marmots of the Deosai plateau, whose burrowing brings up gold-bearing soil that the Minaro (Brokpa) people told him they collected; he suggested a confusion between the Persian words for marmot and "mountain ant". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold-digging_ant · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park . This is Peissel's theory, not settled fact — phrase it as "one theory holds".

## 3. Water

### Sheosar Lake
- At the western end of the park. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake
- Name: "Blind Lake" in Shina (ݜیوسر; *sheo* = blind, *sar* = lake). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake · Dawn 2015 gives the same meaning ("andhi", blind) https://www.dawn.com/news/1178524
- Elevation — sources disagree: **4,250 m** (UNESCO entry https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/ ; Wikipedia) vs **4,142 m** (The Friday Times 2015 https://thefridaytimes.com/16-Jan-2015/the-last-sanctuary ; several travel pages).
- Size: maximum length 2.3 km, maximum width 1.8 km. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake
- Depth: Wikipedia gives an average of 40 m but flags it "citation needed" — see Unconfirmed.
- Coordinates 34°59′29″N 75°14′12″E. Same URL.
- Nanga Parbat can be seen from the lake on a clear day. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake · also visible "from the Astore side" per Dawn 2023 https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028
- Pakistan's submission calls Deosai and Sheosar Lake together one of the highest-elevation freshwater wetlands in the world. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Shoreline is marshy. [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/

### Other lakes and wetlands
- The plateau carries wetlands and numerous small lakes, fed by snow and ice melt from the surrounding mountains. Satellite counts between 1990 and 2019 found 164 to 256 small lakes in any one late-summer image, covering 8.2 to 11.4 km² in total; only 66 lakes were present in every image. Circular small lakes and depressions point to a periglacial (permafrost-influenced) landscape. [peer] Melis et al. 2023 https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/15/7/1800
- Vigne noted two small tarns at the top of the "Sursingeri" ascent in 1835. Vol. 2 p. 229 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002
- A "Marshi Lake" survey block is named in the 2022 government bear survey. https://fwegb.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/GB-FWP-Brown-Bears-Survey-2022-09-03.pdf
- **Ramsar status: none.** Pakistan has 19 Ramsar sites and none is in Gilgit-Baltistan; neither Deosai nor Sheosar is listed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Ramsar_sites_in_Pakistan · count of 19 also in the Ramsar annotated summary (via search snippet) https://rsis.ramsar.org/sites/default/files/rsiswp_search/exports/Ramsar-Sites-annotated-summary-Pakistan.pdf

### Rivers and streams on the plateau
- Three river systems rise in the park: **Shatung**, **Bara Pani** and **Kala Pani**. [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Bara Pani is the largest river crossing the park. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
- A bridge crosses Bara Pani; a road branches from there to Sheosar Lake. https://www.dawn.com/news/1178524
- Shatung Nala lies about 10 km beyond Ali Malik Top on the road in from Skardu, and splits into many winding channels. https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028
- Spelling variants seen: Bara/Barra/Bhara Pani; Shatung Nala/Nullah.
- Literal Urdu meanings (plain translation, not from a cited source): Bara Pani = "big water", Kala Pani = "black water".
- Dwarf willows grow along the rivers and streams. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- The streams hold snow trout that grow very large. Same URL. Vigne in 1835 watched people catching trout by hand in the Deosai streams. Vol. 2 p. 230 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002

### Where the water goes (get this right)
The chain, assembled from sources:
1. Shatung + Bara Pani + Kala Pani join to form the **Shigar River** of Deosai (also spelled Shiggar). [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
2. This Shigar rises on the **Bara Deosai** plateau and flows east to join the **Shingo River**, which itself rises in the **Chhota Deosai** plains (Astore District, north of Minimarg). They meet before the river crosses into Indian-administered Kargil district near Dalunang. [ref] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingo_River
3. At Kaksar (Kargil district) the Shingo joins the **Dras River**. Same URL; also https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_Mountains
4. At Kharul, about 7 km north of Kargil, the **Suru River** joins. Same URL.
5. The combined river flows back into Baltistan (past Olding, Kharmang District) and enters the **Indus** a little above Marol. Same URL (cites Harish Kapadia, *Across Peaks & Passes in Ladakh, Zanskar & East Karakoram*, 1999).

Cautions:
- The UNESCO text calls the Shigar "an important tributary of Indus River" without the intermediate steps. That is true only in the sense that its water reaches the Indus via Shingo → Dras → Suru. Best wording: "the plateau's main streams gather into the Shigar, which joins the Shingo and, by way of the Dras and Suru, reaches the Indus".
- **This is not the Shigar River of the Shigar Valley** north of Skardu (fed by the Baltoro/Braldu side). Wikipedia explicitly warns against confusing the two. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingo_River
- The Shingo River article is tagged as needing more citations; the route rests on one book reference. Good enough for the outline above, not for fine detail.
- Not all Deosai water goes east. The north edge drains to Satpara Lake and the Indus at Skardu (below); the Astore River is also described as one of the rivers draining the Deosai Plateau (it rises on the western slopes of the Burzil Pass). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astore_River
- Vigne (1835) wrote that the plateau's streams send their waters either to the Indus on the north or to the "Jylum" (Jhelum) on the south. Vol. 2 p. 230 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002 . Historical observation only.

### Satpara (Sadpara) Lake and Dam — the Skardu side
- Satpara Lake: elevation 2,600 m; area 4 km²; max length 3.5 km, max width 1.4 km; coordinates 35°13′46″N 75°37′49″E. Fed by snow and glacier melt, with the Deosai Plains named as the main source. Its only outlet, the Satpara Stream, supplies Skardu and drains to the Indus. Also spelled Sadpara (Urdu سدپارہ جھیل). The lake has an island. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satpara_Lake
- Tradition credits the Balti ruler Ali Sher Khan Anchan (r. 1595–1633) with damming the lake. Same URL.
- Vigne described it in the 1830s as "Satpur-Tsuh", filling the whole width of the pass, about 1¾ miles long and nearly a mile wide, with a little willow-covered island. Vol. 2 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002
- Satpara Dam: downstream of the lake on the Satpara Stream, about 4 km from Skardu; 128 ft (39 m) high, 560 ft (170 m) long; installed capacity 17.36 MW; became operational April 2013 (the article is internally inconsistent on construction start: 2003 vs 2009). The dam enlarged the lake. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satpara_Dam
- Shatung diversion: the dam's design included diverting water from the Shatung Nullah, inside Deosai, through a tunnel to the Satpara side to keep the plant running in winter. The diversion was not built with the dam; WAPDA resubmitted a revised plan in November 2018. https://profit.pakistantoday.com.pk/2018/12/02/wapda-decides-to-divert-water-supply-to-satpara-dam (via search snippet). A Heinrich Böll Stiftung discussion paper (Dec 2017) warned of ecological damage to the park and noted no environmental impact assessment had been done. https://www.boell.de/en/2018/07/13/infrastructure-fragile-ecosystems-case-deosai-national-park-gilgit-baltistan-pakistan . Current status (2026) not confirmed.

## 4. Mountains and landscape

### Where it sits
- Western Himalaya, east of Nanga Parbat, close to the Central Karakoram. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- A meeting point of two biogeographic provinces: the Himalayan and the Karakoram–Pamir highlands. Same URL.
- Britannica and Wikipedia class the Deosai Mountains as part of the Himalaya; the range adjoins the Zanskar Range, from which the Suru River separates it. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_Mountains
- Note: the GB Forest Dept page places the park "in the Karakoram range", and Wikipedia's Burji La article puts that pass in the Karakoram. https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/ · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burji_La . Most scientific sources say western Himalaya. Use "western Himalaya, on the edge of the Karakoram".

### Character of the land
- Flat but undulating plains broken by gently rolling hills and ringed by mountains — a sharp contrast with the narrow, steep-walled valleys around it. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Above the timberline; vegetation is herbaceous perennials, grasses and sedges. Four habitat types: marshy, grassy, stony, rocky. Marshy ground (dominated by *Poa* and *Carex*) produces most of the forage; rocky ground is largely bare. [peer] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932756 · [primary] GB bear survey 2022
- Large alpine meadows alternate with drier stony areas. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Vigne, 1835: "A more dreary and desolate-looking tract the sun cannot shine upon." He described granite and gneiss hills rising from the plain and "an horizon of mountain summits" hemming it in on every side, with "Diarmul" (Diamer = Nanga Parbat) sometimes conspicuous in the distance. Vol. 2 p. 229–230 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002

### Peaks seen from or near the plateau
- **Nanga Parbat**: visible from Sheosar Lake on a clear day (Wikipedia, Sheosar Lake); visible from the Astore side (Dawn 2023 https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028); a hill behind the Bara Pani camp gives a view of the plains and Nanga Parbat (Off the Atlas [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/); Vigne saw it from the plateau in 1835 (above).
- **From Burji La** (the pass on the north rim): K2, Nanga Parbat, Masherbrum, Chogolisa, Laila Peak, Golden Peak (Spantik), Gasherbrum I, II and IV, and part of Broad Peak. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burji_La . The Central Karakoram National Park site lists K2, Nanga Parbat, Spantik, Broad Peak, Latok, Masherbrum and Gasherbrum I–IV from "Burgeh La". https://www.cknp.org/k2-view-points/ . Clear weather is essential.

### Geology and origin
- Rocks: the Deosai Mountains are mainly Precambrian sedimentary rock with younger granite bodies. [ref] Britannica (via search snippet) · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_Mountains . Vigne recorded granite and gneiss in 1835.
- A very old surface: thermochronology shows the plateau has been wearing down slowly and continuously, at less than 250 m per million years, for at least 35 million years. The authors conclude it has been stable since at least the Eocene and is a preserved remnant of a once larger south-western Tibetan plateau. They argue this contradicts the idea that such flat high surfaces were carved by Ice Age glaciers. [peer] van der Beek et al. 2009, *Nature Geoscience* 2: 364–368 https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo503 (author PDF: https://www.isterre.fr/IMG/pdf/vanderbeek-nature-2009-p1.pdf)
- Glaciation: Kuhle (2004) presents field evidence for the maximum last-glacial (Würm, about 60–18 thousand years ago) ice cover of the Karakoram and the Deosai Plateau, reconstructing a huge connected ice-stream network feeding an Indus glacier. [peer] https://egqsj.copernicus.org/articles/54/95/2004/ . So: the plateau was ice-covered in the last glacial period, but (per van der Beek) the flat surface itself is far older than the ice. Present both; do not write "carved by glaciers".
- Today there are no glaciers on the plateau comparable to neighbouring parks — UNESCO's comparison notes Deosai lacks the high peaks and glaciers of India's Great Himalayan National Park. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Permafrost: global maps indicate continuous/discontinuous permafrost along the high rim; the 2023 satellite study looked for signs of permafrost change in the lake and wetland record (small lakes slightly decreasing, wetland area increasing 1990–2019). [peer] https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/15/7/1800

### Soils
- Severely eroded, coarse, mixed with gravel and stones; deeper soils with marshy vegetation in the flats between hills. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
- Alpine soils of this kind are coarse, stony, weakly mineralised and high in organic carbon because decomposition is slow. [peer] Shaheen et al. 2019 https://www.pakbs.org/pjbot/papers/1538435113.pdf

Safe wording: "The road across is usually open only from about June to September or October; dates shift with the snow each year."

### Snow depth
- 6–8 m in winter. [peer] Shaheen et al. 2019 https://www.pakbs.org/pjbot/papers/1538435113.pdf (the only scholarly figure found; high, and unsourced within the paper)
- 1835: "five or six yards deep" (roughly 4.5–5.5 m). Vigne vol. 2 p. 219. Rashid cites this as about five metres and says it is no longer the case. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
- Villagers at Chillum say snowfall has fallen sharply over 30 years: village snow of two to three metres then, rarely more than a metre now. https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/

### Temperature
- Mean daily temperatures range from −20 °C to 12 °C. [peer] Nawaz et al. 2019 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932756 · Melis et al. 2023 · GB bear survey 2022
- Variant: −20 °C in winter to 9 °C in summer. [peer] Shaheen et al. 2019
- Summer pasture temperatures usually 6–8 °C, according to a Bakarwal herder. https://dialogue.earth/en/nature/bakarwal-community-faces-dual-threat-pakistan/
- Summer nights can fall below freezing; ice or snow on the tent in the morning is possible. [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/
- 5 September 1835: Vigne's thermometer read 43 °F (6 °C) inside his tent and the ground was white with hoar-frost at 7 a.m. Vol. 2 https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002
- Vigne was told the winter cold was "too piercing for the existence of animal life". Same volume.

### Precipitation — two different ranges
| Value | Source |
|---|---|
| 350–550 mm a year, mostly as winter snow | UNESCO entry [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/ · DOAM |
| 510–750 mm a year, mostly as snow | Nawaz et al. 2019 [peer] https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932756 · Shaheen 2019 · Melis 2023 · GB bear survey 2022 (all trace to Nawaz) |

- Deosai gets more rain than the valleys of Baltistan to its north and north-east because it faces the monsoon-affected outer Himalaya. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- For contrast, Gilgit-Baltistan as a whole averages only about 200 mm a year (Pakistan Meteorological Department figure quoted by Dialogue Earth). https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/

### The thaw
- 1835: from about 15 June the sun turns the whole plain into flood for 25–30 days, making it impassable. Vigne vol. 2 p. 219.
- June 1990: Salman Rashid found streams so swollen with melt that he and his porters walked to their headwaters to cross; they met no other humans in five days. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
- In summer "a very large number of rivers flow". [peer] Melis et al. 2023

### Growing season and flowers
- Short summer season, mid-May to September. [peer] Shaheen et al. 2019
- Gujjar folklore puts the true summer at barely eight weeks. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
- Wildflowers and rare butterflies cover the plateau "in the Spring season" (which at this altitude means early summer). https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Late July is given as peak wildflower time; grass does not last past September. [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/
- A July visitor described flowers of every colour along the waterways. https://www.dawn.com/news/1767848 · https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028
- Flora: one 2016 survey recorded 132 plant species in 101 genera and 41 families. [peer] Shaheen et al. 2019. About 150 medicinal plants have been identified in the park, per the GB Forest Conservator. https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/
- No source gave a measured growing-season length in days.

### Bears and the seasons
- The bears den mainly in the surrounding valleys; they enter the park in June and leave in early October when the snow returns. [peer] Nawaz et al. 2019 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932756
- General (not Deosai-specific) statement: Himalayan brown bears hibernate from around October and emerge in April–May. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayan_brown_bear (via search snippet)

### Change
- Local herders and officials report less snowfall, snow arriving later (spring instead of January–February), shorter summers and thinner grass. Snow fell in parts of Deosai in July 2018 and had not fully melted by late July 2020. https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/ · https://dialogue.earth/en/nature/bakarwal-community-faces-dual-threat-pakistan/ . These are reported testimony, not measurements.

## 6. Sky

Documented statements are thin. What can be supported:

- Pakistan's UNESCO submission describes the plateau environment as extreme cold with low atmospheric pressure, low oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, aridity, and intense, rapid solar ultraviolet radiation. [primary] https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Alpine plants here are adapted to high wind velocity, blizzards and high UV (general statement about the alpine zone in the Deosai flora paper). [peer] https://www.pakbs.org/pjbot/papers/1538435113.pdf
- Weather is highly changeable: snow can fall in summer, sunshine alternates with overcast, with icy winds and thunderstorms. [news] Dawn 2015 https://www.dawn.com/news/1178524 . A July 2023 visitor met light snowfall at Sheosar Lake. https://www.dawn.com/news/1767848
- Night sky: the only first-hand statement found is a travel guide whose author says the stars are extremely visible and the open terrain makes the sky look enormous, and advises warm clothing for night photography. [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/
- A Pamir Times photo feature includes a Milky Way image in its Deosai set, without saying exactly where it was taken. https://pamirtimes.net/2017/09/28/pictorial-journeying-through-the-mesmerizing-gilgit-baltistan-region-of-pakistan/ (via search snippet)
- No permanent settlement exists on the plateau (UNESCO), which supports a plain statement that there is no town lighting on the plains. Do not go further than that.

Not found: any dark-sky designation, any sky-brightness (Bortle/SQM) measurement, any observatory or astronomy-society statement. See Unconfirmed.

## 7. People and access

### Routes and distances
- Two road approaches: from Skardu via Sadpara (north-east) and from Astore via Chilam (west). One unpaved road links them across the plateau. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/
- Official Astore district brochure, cumulative distances on "Astore to Skardu via Deosai": Chilim Chauki 53 km, Sheosar Lake 64 km, Bara Pani 99 km, Sadpara 107 km, Skardu 115 km. [primary] https://tourism.gov.pk/publications/DISTRICT%20ASTORE.pdf . (The last two intervals look short against other sources; use the 115 km total and the Chilim/Sheosar figures with attribution.)
- Skardu is about 30 km from the park, the shortest approach. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
- Sadpara village is about 7 km from Skardu; a road runs from the village to "Sadpara top" on the plateau, about 1.5 hours by jeep or about 6 hours on foot. https://www.cknp.org/k2-view-points/
- Skardu to Deosai: nearly two and a half hours' drive (Dawn June 2023 https://www.dawn.com/news/1760028). Crossing the plateau takes about 3–4 hours (Off the Atlas).
- Astore town to Chilam: about four hours by jeep; Chilam is the last settlement before the plains. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake
- Chillum Sherkoli (Astore district), 3,600 m, about 800 people, is the closest settlement to the park. https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/
- April 2026: the federal government approved road projects including a 54 km Astore–Chilam Chowki road and a 31 km Deosai–Skardu road. https://www.pakistantoday.com.pk/2026/04/17/federal-govt-announces-new-road-projects-in-gb
- Campsites named by a travel guide: Bara Pani (most facilities), Kala Pani (about 45 minutes on), Sheosar (most basic). [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/ — but see the 2025 restrictions below.

### Herders and grazing
- Gujjar-Bakarwal transhumant herders come up from the lowlands every summer and use the plateau as grazing land. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park · [primary] GB bear survey 2022 · https://www.dawn.com/news/1164658
- A Gujjar elder told Salman Rashid in the mid-1990s that his clan and their herds spent summers on Deosai and that several Gujjar groups held claimed grazing areas. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
- Bakarwals winter on the plains (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Punjab, Azad Kashmir) and move in the warm months to high meadows in Azad Kashmir, Swat and Deosai; one herder described spending four months on the Deosai pastures and reaching the Punjab plains at the end of October. https://dialogue.earth/en/nature/bakarwal-community-faces-dual-threat-pakistan/
- Nomads from Kashmir have crossed the plains with their herds for centuries. https://www.dawn.com/news/1178524
- Local grazing rights — figures differ: restricted to seven communities in designated sectors (UNESCO entry https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/); nearly fifty neighbouring communities hold traditional grazing rights (Shaheen et al. 2019). The 2022 survey names Sadpara, Shilla, Dappa and Karabosh as valleys with rights in the core area.
- 2022 government survey — the report's own summary: 19 camping sites housing about 244 people, and about 4,834 head of livestock, in the park and buffer valleys. https://fwegb.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/GB-FWP-Brown-Bears-Survey-2022-09-03.pdf . The Express Tribune (Oct 2026) reports the same survey as 12 campsites, 66 tents and 173 people inside Deosai, plus about 4,834 livestock. https://tribune.com.pk/story/2633041/rare-brown-bears-face-new-threat-in-deosai . Use the report's figures and say "in the park and its buffer valleys".
- Off the Atlas also names Brokpa herders alongside Bakarwal and Gujjar. [travel]

### Park rules and pressures
- December 2025: the GB Forest, Parks and Wildlife Department banned seasonal hotels, temporary guest accommodation and other commercial ventures inside the park for the coming season; visitors must use facilities outside the boundary. Camping had already been prohibited in parts of Deosai after a September 2025 incident in which a singer was injured by a brown bear. https://propakistani.pk/2025/12/18/govt-bans-hotels-and-businesses-at-popular-tourist-spot/ . Check the current season's rules before printing anything about camping.
- The park has a core zone specially protected for bears and zoned grazing areas; HWF's programme set up check posts, patrols and an entry-fee system and targeted bear hunting, illegal fishing and falcon trapping. https://hwf.org.pk/?p=2025
- Entry fee for foreigners reported as 3,100 PKR (2021–23 guide; likely out of date). [travel] https://offtheatlas.com/visiting-deosai-national-park/
- Vehicle pressure: around 5,000–6,000 vehicles a month entering in season, per a GB Environmental Protection Agency official in 2018. https://dialogue.earth/en/climate/climate-change-hits-herders-on-himalayan-meadow/
- Litter and food waste draw bears; plastic fragments have been seen in bear droppings (Express Tribune, Oct 2026). https://tribune.com.pk/story/2633041/rare-brown-bears-face-new-threat-in-deosai

### History
- Historically an important route between Kashmir and Gilgit, and a place where tribal disputes were settled before Partition; today it lies near the Line of Control. [primary] https://fwegb.gov.pk/national-parks/
- 1637: Zafar Khan, Mughal governor of Kashmir, marched about 2,000 cavalry and 10,000 infantry against "Little Tibet" (Baltistan) and fought at Sadpara; Salman Rashid infers the army crossed Deosai, though no chronicle says so. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945 . Present as Rashid's inference.
- 1835: Godfrey Thomas Vigne crossed "Deotsuh" from Burzil to Skardu in the company of Ahmed Shah, ruler of Skardu, the day after the Raja's force destroyed a band of robbers; the party had swelled to seven or eight hundred people. Vigne published it in *Travels in Kashmir, Ladak, Iskardo…* (London: Henry Colburn, 1842), vol. 2 pp. 219–231. https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002
  - Direct quotes available (public domain): "A more dreary and desolate-looking tract the sun cannot shine upon." · "Bears are very numerous upon Deotsuh" · the marmot there was "as large as a small fox", known by its "loud, shrill, and repeated whistle".
- Vigne is also credited as the first European to describe Nanga Parbat. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Godfrey_Vigne (via search snippet)
- 1947–48: Wikipedia's Deosai Mountains article carries a photo caption saying Gilgit Scouts crossed the Deosai Mountains during the First Kashmir War in November 1947. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_Mountains . A Pamir Times tribute says Lt Shah Khan's "Eskimo Force" marched over Deosai in waist-deep snow in mid-winter. https://pamirtimes.net/2018/04/10/in-remembrance-group-captain-retd-mohammad-shah-khan-sitara-e-juraat/ (via search snippet). Thin sourcing — see Unconfirmed.

### Conservation story
- In the late 1980s (Wikipedia's HWF article says 1987) Vaqar Zakaria and Anis ur Rahman went looking for bears on Deosai and found none on that visit. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Himalayan_Wildlife_Foundation · https://thefridaytimes.com/17-Jan-2014/land-of-the-giant
- 1993: the Himalayan Wildlife Project (later Himalayan Wildlife Foundation) set up a research camp — the first scientific study and census of the bears. The same year the boundary was demarcated and the area protected. https://www.dawn.com/news/1164658
- First count: 19 bears in 1993, far fewer than the wildlife department had claimed. [peer] Nawaz et al. 2008 https://www.currentconservation.org/recovery-of-brown-bears-in-northern-pakistan/ . (A WWF official in Dawn 2003 said 16 in 1993; Dialogue Earth quotes WWF as 19 "in 1991". Use 19 in 1993.)
- Growth: 43 by 2006, about 5% a year, credited to fewer human-caused deaths and community participation (Nawaz et al. 2008); about 56 in 2013 and an estimated 66 (95% range 58–77) in the park and buffer valleys in 2022 (GB survey 2022). UNESCO's 2016 text says 72. Wikipedia's "78 (2022)" does not match the survey's central estimate.
- Deosai holds the only stable brown bear population in the region; the others do not exceed about 20 animals each. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
- Documentaries: *Deosai – The Last Sanctuary* (Walkabout Films, 2015); *Deosai – Wilderness in Peril* (UNDP and Walkabout Films). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
- Scholarly references worth citing by name: Nawaz, Swenson & Zakaria (2008), *Biological Conservation* 141(9): 2230–2241, doi:10.1016/j.biocon.2008.06.012; Nawaz (2007), *Ursus*, "Status of the brown bear in Pakistan".

## 8. Facts worth telling

1. **The plateau is older than the Himalaya's present shape.** Rock-cooling ages show Deosai's surface has eroded at under 250 m per million years for at least 35 million years — a leftover scrap of an ancient, larger Tibetan plateau, not something the Ice Age carved. (van der Beek et al. 2009, *Nature Geoscience*) https://www.nature.com/articles/ngeo503
2. **An 1835 eyewitness hated it.** G. T. Vigne: "A more dreary and desolate-looking tract the sun cannot shine upon." In the same pages he notes that bears are "very numerous" — 158 years before the park was created to save them. https://archive.org/details/b29350402_0002
3. **Frost in the tent on 5 September.** Vigne's thermometer read 43 °F inside his tent with hoar-frost on the ground at 7 a.m. Same source.
4. **The plain used to drown itself every June.** Vigne was told that from mid-June the melt flooded the whole plain for 25–30 days, and guessed the name meant "lake of the Deo". Same source, p. 219.
5. **One giant, not many.** In the Gujjar telling the name is *Dev Vasai*, the land a single simple-minded giant tried to farm until the early snow beat him; a fox had warned him the summer lasts barely eight weeks. https://www.dawn.com/news/1964945
6. **The Balti name is a calendar.** Ghbiarsa — "summer's place" — because that is the only time you can get there. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deosai_National_Park
7. **Herodotus' gold-digging ants may be Deosai's marmots.** Michel Peissel's 1984 theory links the "fox-sized ants" of the *Histories* to the plateau's burrowing marmots. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold-digging_ant
8. **Two hundred lakes that come and go.** Satellite images from 1990–2019 show between 164 and 256 small lakes each late summer, but only 66 appear every time. (Melis et al. 2023) https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/15/7/1800
9. **Its water crosses a disputed border and comes back.** The plateau's streams gather into the Shigar, join the Shingo, pass through Kargil with the Dras and Suru, and re-enter Baltistan to meet the Indus near Marol. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shingo_River
10. **Wetter than its neighbours.** Because it faces the monsoon-touched outer Himalaya, Deosai gets more precipitation than the Baltistan valleys just to its north. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
11. **Nineteen bears.** That was the 1993 count; the 2022 government survey estimated 66 in the park and its buffer valleys. https://www.currentconservation.org/recovery-of-brown-bears-in-northern-pakistan/ · https://fwegb.gov.pk/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/GB-FWP-Brown-Bears-Survey-2022-09-03.pdf
12. **The bears are summer visitors too.** They den mostly in the valleys around the plateau, arriving in June and leaving in early October. (Nawaz et al. 2019) https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932756
13. **From one pass on the rim you can see K2 and Nanga Parbat.** Burji La's panorama takes in K2, Nanga Parbat, Masherbrum, the Gasherbrums and Broad Peak in clear weather. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burji_La · https://www.cknp.org/k2-view-points/
14. **Nobody lives there.** No permanent human habitation on the plains. https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6115/
15. **Sheosar means "Blind Lake"** in Shina. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheosar_Lake
