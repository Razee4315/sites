The short version

A bell is the only instrument that is cast, not built. There is one pour and it lasts four minutes. Whatever comes out of the ground will hang in a tower and be heard by people who are not born yet. We have done this 4,117 times. We still do not sleep the night before.

Chapter I — The making

It takes four months, of which four minutes matter.

i. Loam

First we build the silence.

A bell begins as the space where a bell will be. We shape it twice in loam, which is clay, sand, horse manure and goat hair: an inner core, an outer cope, and between them a gap the exact shape of the sound. The recipe has not changed since 1687. Neither has the smell.

ii. Fire

77 : 23 Copper Tin

Bronze so hard it is nearly glass.

More tin and it shatters. Less and it thuds. We melt four tonnes overnight at 1,150 °C and skim it with a willow pole, because willow is what Hester Toller used in 1790 and nobody has dared try anything else.

iv. Patience

Nine days in the ground.

Cool a bell too quickly and it cracks the first time it is struck. So it stays buried in the pit, in its own heat, for nine days. We have tried being impatient. The result is in the yard, in two pieces, where the apprentices can see it.

Chapter III — Tuning

One bell. Five notes.

Strike a bell and you hear a chord, not a note. Five separate tones sound at once, and in an untuned bell they disagree. The tuner's whole job is to make them agree. She does it on a vertical lathe built in 1921, taking shavings off the inside a fraction of a millimetre at a time, and listening.

Chapter IV — The ring

Ring the changes.

Hang six bells in a tower and the English will not play a tune on them. They will ring them in every possible order instead, by rule, from memory, never repeating a row. It is called change ringing. It is four centuries old, closer to mathematics than to music, and a full peal takes three hours without stopping.

Press 1–6, tap a bell, or let us ring for you.

Five kinds of bell. One kind of metal.
