Supper Nº 42

Sat. 14 November, at dusk

Bakenessergracht 91

Haarlem, NL

A still life, until you sit down.

Once a month we lay one table the way Willem Heda painted his in 1648. Twelve guests arrive at dusk. By dawn there is nothing left to paint.

Scroll to begin eating

pronk /prɔŋk/ Dutch, noun & verb. Display; to show off. A pronkstilleven is a still life painted to flaunt what the owner could afford to let go cold.

For four hundred years the Dutch painted suppers that nobody was allowed to eat. The lemon stayed half-peeled. The oysters stayed open. The glass stayed full. We think that is a terrible waste of a lemon.

  1. I

    Composed

    Every table is arranged by a painter, object by object, over two days. Light from the left. One thing always about to fall.

  2. II

    Portrayed

    At twenty to six the finished table sits for its portrait. That picture is the only menu you will get, and it hangs in the hall for a month.

  3. III

    Ruined

    At six the painter leaves the room and twelve of you sit down. Nobody serves. Nobody clears. What you can see is what there is.

  1. Portrait taken 17:40

  2. IFirst course — 18:00

    Oysters, a lemon, Rhine wine

    Six Zeeland flats on pewter. The lemon arrives half-peeled, as it has been since 1648. You are the first person in four centuries permitted to finish the job.

    Left on the table — six shells, one rind

  3. IISecond course — 19:20

    Bread, butter, two herrings

    A sober course, as the Calvinists would have wanted. Torn, not sliced. The knife has been hanging over the edge of the table all evening, asking to be picked up.

    Left on the table — two backbones, a heel of crust

  4. IIIThird course — 21:05

    The lobster

    Boiled at five, painted at twenty to six, eaten at nine. In the old pictures it stood for a world turned backwards. Here it means roll up your sleeves.

    Left on the table — armour

  5. IVFourth course — 23:30

    A raised pie

    Hare, prunes and mace behind a wall of pastry built to be looked at. It took two days to raise. It takes about eleven minutes to bring down.

    Left on the table — rubble, one spoon

  6. VFifth course — 02:10

    Cheese, fruit, whatever is left

    The candle is a third of itself and nobody is using a plate. Figs, grapes, a Gouda old enough to vote. Somebody will knock over the glass. Somebody always does.

    Left on the table — stalks, stones, shells

  7. Second portrait taken 05:52

    The table, after you

    The candle is out. So, more or less, are you.

05:52 — Dawn

Nothing left to paint.

We open the shutters, put out bread and the butter you did not finish, and paint the table once more: the same picture, twelve hours older. That one you take home.

The Inventory — ten lots

Nothing on a Dutch table was only food.

Every object was a small sermon about how short life is and how badly you are spending it. We have kept the objects and edited the sermons.

The House

One room, one table, twelve chairs.

A merchant’s house on the Bakenessergracht, built in 1642 and not much improved since. The window faces west, for the light at twenty to six. There is no kitchen you can see and no music you can hear.

House rules

  1. 1

    Arrive at dusk. The table is still sitting for its portrait. You may look. You may not touch.

  2. 2

    There is no menu. If you can see it, you can eat it.

  3. 3

    No phones on the linen. The table has been painted enough for one day.

  4. 4

    Nobody clears. What is eaten stays where it fell.

  5. 5

    We end when the candle does.
